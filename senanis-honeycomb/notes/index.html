Senanis Honeycomb Notes

How the Senanis Honeycomb opens into paths

A Senanis Honeycomb grows from the six-word Hexagon and can expand to many different sizes. Each new cell joins the wider field along shared edges. Every cell holds a Senanis Moment, while a shared edge lets two neighboring Moments meet through the same pair of words.

This particular Honeycomb is the smallest form with a complete ring around a center: six hexagons gather around a seventh. It contains 7 hexagonal cells, 24 unique word vertices, and 30 edges. A reader can begin at any word and follow connected, unvisited vertices to make a six-word Moment that crosses the boundaries between cells.

The same topology supports longer readings. A Hamiltonian path visits every vertex exactly once, producing a complete twenty-four-word poem without repeating a word. This Honeycomb has 72 directed readings, or 36 paths when a reading and its reverse are treated as the same path.

These complete readings take three forms: they can move from the inner hexagon to the outer boundary, from the boundary into the inner hexagon, or from the boundary through the center and back out to the remaining boundary. In every case, the six words of the inner hexagon remain an uninterrupted passage within the longer poem.

The form also draws inspiration from fourth-century Chinese poet Su Hui’sStar Gauge (Xuanji Tu), a poem arranged as a field of characters that can be followed along many routes. The Honeycomb uses a different geometry and a much smaller vocabulary, but shares the idea that spatial arrangement can hold many poems at once.

The six-word exploration keeps those possibilities in the reader’s hands. The generated readings reveal another scale of the same structure: complete poems discovered through the topology rather than imposed as a single fixed order. Larger Honeycombs can extend both possibilities, opening longer and more intricate routes through their words.
